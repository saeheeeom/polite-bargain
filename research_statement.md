# Research Statement (Draft)

## Title
**"The Price of Politeness: Language and Persona in LLM Negotiation Agents"**

*Workspace/codename: `polite-bargain`*

## Abstract

LLM-based negotiation agents are increasingly proposed for multilingual, customer-facing deployment, yet prior work on LLM negotiation has studied persona and prompting strategy almost exclusively within English. We present a controlled study of price-negotiation agents built on the CraigslistBargain dataset, crossing **language** (English, Korean) with **seller urgency** (none, strong need for cash) and **buyer persona** (cooperative, neutral, headstrong) in a 2×2×3 factorial design. Agents output both natural-language utterances and explicit structured actions (offer/accept/reject/counter-offer) each turn, and negotiation outcomes (agreement rate, final price, turn count) are compared across all 12 conditions. To explain *why* outcomes might differ by language, we code every negotiation turn for Face-Threatening Acts and politeness-mitigation strategy (Brown & Levinson, 1987), interpreted alongside Ting-Toomey's cross-cultural Face-Negotiation Theory (1985), and test whether this strategy distribution mediates the observed outcome differences — including whether persona effects themselves interact with language. We report this as a pilot-scale case study (English-Korean only, ~430 negotiations), motivated in part by the complete absence of any existing Korean-language negotiation dataset. Our aim is to surface early evidence on whether multilingual deployment of LLM negotiation agents risks systematically different real-world outcomes purely as a function of interaction language.

## Background: What This Study Is Comparing

Two LLM agents are given a price negotiation scenario (a buyer and a seller, each with a target price and an item to negotiate over) and left to negotiate the deal on their own, turn by turn, until they reach an agreement or give up. Each turn, an agent outputs both a natural-language utterance **and** an explicit structured action (offer / accept / reject / counter-offer, plus a price) — mirroring the `dialogue_acts` structure already present in the underlying dataset. This structured-action requirement is what makes this genuinely an *agent* rather than freeform dialogue role-play: at every turn, the model must make and declare an explicit decision (hold firm, concede, accept, walk away), not just continue a conversation.

Two independent manipulations are layered on top of this base setup:
1. **Language** — the entire negotiation is conducted in English or Korean.
2. **Seller urgency** — the seller's system prompt signals no urgency, weak urgency, or strong urgency (need cash quickly).
3. **Buyer persona** — the buyer is assigned one of three negotiating personas: **cooperative** (prioritizes reaching a mutually workable deal quickly, concedes readily), **neutral** (no explicit disposition beyond the base negotiation instructions — this also serves as a baseline), or **headstrong** (anchors hard on the target price, concedes slowly and reluctantly).

The novelty isn't the negotiation setup itself (well-established in prior LLM agent research), but layering **language** on top of persona as a *second* source of variation and explicitly testing whether they **interact** — i.e., does a given persona (say, cooperative) play out differently in Korean than in English, and if so, does that interaction actually move the final negotiated price?

## Why This Is Needed

Prior LLM negotiation research has focused almost entirely on persona and prompting strategy as the variable of interest. Whether the *language itself* changes negotiation behavior remains largely unexamined — despite the fact that languages differ substantially in how formality, directness, and politeness are grammatically encoded. Korean, for example, requires speakers to grammatically mark formality in nearly every sentence (via verb conjugation, particles, and pronoun choice), while English encodes politeness mostly through lexical choice rather than obligatory grammar. If an LLM's negotiation behavior is shaped by these built-in linguistic conventions, that has real implications for anyone deploying multilingual negotiation or customer-facing agents — the same underlying "strategy" prompt could produce systematically different real-world outcomes depending on the deployment language.

This gap is not just theoretical: a search across major public dataset repositories (Hugging Face, AI Hub) and known releases from Naver, Kakao, and NVIDIA turned up no existing Korean-language negotiation/bargaining dataset — every established negotiation benchmark (CraigslistBargain, Deal-or-No-Deal, CaSiNo) is English-only, and Korean dialogue datasets that do exist (AI Hub's customer service/complaint dialogues, persona-chat corpora, etc.) do not involve two parties with conflicting goals reaching a negotiated agreement. The one close multilingual analogue, X-RiSAWOZ, covers task-oriented booking dialogue in Korean and Hindi but not competitive bargaining. The complete absence of a Korean negotiation resource is itself part of the motivation for this study: without it, there is currently no way to even ask whether Korean LLM negotiation behavior differs from English, let alone why.

There's also a specific gap around *persona*: prior work (e.g., "LLMs with Personalities in Multi-issue Negotiation Games," see References) has shown persona affects negotiation outcomes, but always within a single language. Whether a given persona's effect is stable across languages, or whether the same "headstrong" instruction produces a differently-sized effect in Korean versus English, is an open question this study is positioned to answer directly by crossing the two factors rather than studying either alone.

## Hypotheses

1. Deal outcomes (agreement rate, final price relative to target price, number of dialogue turns) differ systematically between languages, even when the scenario, target prices, and buyer persona are held identical.
2. These outcome differences are mediated by measurable stylistic differences — e.g., directness of opening offers, rate and size of concessions, and politeness markers — that vary by language due to differences in how formality is grammatically encoded.
3. Introducing a seller-side urgency/need frame (the seller needs cash quickly) increases the size and speed of concessions in both languages, but the *size* of this effect differs by language — with Korean's stronger grammaticalized politeness/empathy conventions predicted to make the effect either amplified (harder to press an already-empathized-with counterpart) or dampened (harder to state the ask directly at all) relative to English. The direction is treated as an open empirical question rather than a directional prediction, since the theoretical case could point either way.
4. Buyer persona (cooperative / neutral / headstrong) affects negotiation outcomes as expected on its own (e.g., headstrong buyers achieve lower final prices), but **the size of the persona effect itself differs by language** — i.e., language and persona interact rather than simply adding independent effects. For example, a "headstrong" instruction may be harder to fully express through Korean's grammaticalized politeness norms, producing a smaller behavioral gap between headstrong and neutral personas in Korean than in English.

## Literature Review

**LLM negotiation agents.** LLM-vs-LLM negotiation as a research paradigm traces back to pre-LLM work like Lewis et al.'s *Deal or No Deal?* (2017), which trained end-to-end negotiation dialogue agents, and He et al.'s *Decoupling Strategy and Generation in Negotiation Dialogues* (2018), which introduced the CraigslistBargain dataset this study builds on. More recent work has moved to prompting/role-play with pretrained LLMs directly: *AgreeMate: Teaching LLMs to Haggle* (2024) benchmarks LLMs on CraigslistBargain using agreement rate and price-achieved metrics, which this study adopts as its own outcome measures for comparability. Hua et al.'s *Assistive Large Language Model Agents for Socially-Aware Negotiation Dialogues* (2024) extends the negotiation-agent paradigm with a third "remediator" agent that intervenes to improve social outcomes — a useful methodological reference for how this workshop community frames multi-agent negotiation studies, though its focus (intervention) differs from this study's focus (comparison across conditions). Most directly relevant is *LLMs with Personalities in Multi-issue Negotiation Games* (accepted at SocialLLM @ ICWSM 2026 / Social Agents in the Wild @ ACL 2026, the sister editions of this study's target workshop), which establishes that persona measurably affects LLM negotiation outcomes — but entirely within a single language, leaving open exactly the language×persona interaction question this study investigates.

**Cross-lingual and cross-cultural LLM behavior.** A growing body of work shows LLM behavior is not language-neutral even holding task and model constant. *Better To Ask in English?* (2025) finds factual accuracy itself varies by language for the same underlying task. More directly relevant to this study's design, *Should We Respect LLMs? A Cross-Lingual Study on the Influence of Prompt Politeness on LLM Performance* (Yin et al., SICon 2024) finds that prompt politeness affects LLM task performance differently across English, Chinese, and Japanese — the "best" politeness level is language-dependent, not universal. This finding is extended and strengthened by *No Universal Courtesy: A Cross-Linguistic, Multi-Model Study of Politeness Effects on LLMs Using the PLUM Corpus* (2026), which tests English, Hindi, and Spanish across multiple model families and confirms politeness effects are language- *and* model-dependent, releasing the PLUM corpus (1,500 human-validated prompts across 3 languages, 5 politeness categories) as a resource. Both papers study politeness as an input variable affecting task *performance*; this study differs by studying politeness as an *outcome* — i.e., how politeness/directness is itself produced differently by language, and whether that produced difference in turn affects a downstream social/economic outcome (negotiated price) rather than an accuracy metric. This is a meaningful distinction to state clearly in the paper, since it positions the contribution as complementary to, not duplicating, the Yin et al. and PLUM lines of work.

**Judge reliability across languages.** Since this study relies on an LLM judge to code FTA/politeness strategies, judge reliability itself is a relevant risk, and there is direct evidence it's a real concern: *Cross-Lingual Stability of LLM Judges Under Controlled Generation: Evidence from Finno-Ugric Languages* (Chung & Freienthal, 2026) holds generation conditions constant while varying target language (Estonian, Finnish, Hungarian) and finds that while surface-level metrics stay stable across languages, *pragmatic* judgments — coherence, instruction-following — show rank instabilities and near-zero cross-language correlation. This is a close methodological cousin of this study's own judge-based FTA coding (also a pragmatic/discourse-level judgment, not a surface metric), and directly motivates the manual spot-check of judge scores already planned under Weaknesses — this paper should be cited there as concrete evidence for *why* that spot-check is necessary, not just a generic caution.

**Politeness theory and computational face-act modeling.** The cause-analysis methodology (see Method) draws on Brown & Levinson's *Politeness: Some Universals in Language Usage* (1987), the foundational framework for Face-Threatening Acts and politeness-mitigation strategies, and on Ting-Toomey's *Face-Negotiation Theory* (1985) as a cross-culturally-designed complement addressing B&L's Western-centrism critique. *Keeping Up Appearances: Computational Modeling of Face Acts in Persuasion Oriented Discussions* (2020) provides a direct computational precedent for operationalizing this theory as an NLP coding scheme, which this study's judge-model-based coding pipeline follows.

**Multilingual dataset construction and translation methodology.** Since no Korean negotiation dataset exists (confirmed by direct search across Hugging Face, AI Hub, and major Korean tech company releases), this study must construct its own Korean materials via translation. *X-RiSAWOZ* (2023) is the closest existing precedent — a multilingual (including Korean and Hindi) dialogue dataset built via machine translation plus human post-editing — and *BenchMAX* (2025) formalizes this translate-then-verify approach with native-speaker post-editing cross-checked against automatic verifiers. This study adopts a deliberately lighter-weight version of that pipeline (spot-checking rather than full item-by-item verification, given the small fixed translation surface — 6 item categories, 1 urgency sentence, 3 persona descriptions), a tradeoff explicitly named as a limitation.

## Experimental Design

*(Summary — see the full Method section below for complete detail.)*

- **Design type:** 2 (language: English, Korean) × 2 (seller urgency: none, strong) × 3 (buyer persona: cooperative, neutral, headstrong) full factorial = 12 conditions.
- **Unit of analysis:** a single negotiation (one scenario, run once under one of the 12 conditions).
- **Sample:** 12 scenarios × 12 conditions × 3 repetitions = 432 negotiations total.
- **Independent variables:** language, seller urgency, buyer persona (all three manipulated/assigned, not observed).
- **Dependent variables (outcome):** agreement rate, final price (as a fraction of each side's target), number of turns to resolution.
- **Dependent variables (process/mechanism):** per-turn FTA presence and politeness-mitigation strategy category (4-way: bald on-record, positive politeness, negative politeness, off-record), coded post-hoc per full transcript.
- **Primary comparisons:** (1) main effect of language on outcomes; (2) language × urgency interaction; (3) language × persona interaction (the study's central novel test); (4) whether the politeness-strategy distribution mediates any of the above outcome differences.
- **Models:** Google Gemini (primary, both agents) and Qwen3 via OpenRouter (secondary model for a generalizability check on core findings); a model different from the negotiating model(s) is used as the FTA-coding judge.
- **Scope caveat:** with 12 scenarios × 3 repetitions per cell, this is a pilot-scale exploratory design, underpowered for confidently detecting the three-way interaction it is designed to probe — findings should be reported as suggestive, not confirmatory.

## Method

- **Dataset:** `stanfordnlp/craigslist_bargains` (Hugging Face) — 6,682 real buyer/seller negotiation scenarios (item descriptions, listing prices, buyer target prices at 0.5x/0.7x/0.9x of listing price). Fields confirmed directly from the dataset schema: `items` (Category, Title, Description, Price), `agent_info` (Role, Target, Bottomline), `dialogue_acts` (intent, price per turn), `utterance`. No urgency, need, seller-circumstance, or persona field exists in the dataset — both urgency and persona are conditions the study adds on top of the existing scenarios, not something mined from the data, and this should be stated explicitly in the paper's method section.
- **Languages:** English and Korean only (dropped the planned third language for now, to keep scope manageable within the timeline).
- **Urgency conditions (2 levels, added by the researchers, not present in the source data):** **None** (baseline, original CraigslistBargain framing) vs. **Strong** ("I need the cash quickly," appended to the seller's system prompt). *Reduced from the originally planned 3 levels (dropping "weak") specifically to make room for the new persona factor below without the total condition count becoming unmanageable — see the design/budget note.*
- **Buyer persona conditions (3 levels, added by the researchers, not present in the source data):**
  1. **Cooperative** — prioritizes reaching a mutually workable deal quickly; concedes readily.
  2. **Neutral** — no explicit disposition beyond the base negotiation instructions; serves as the baseline.
  3. **Headstrong** — anchors hard on the target price; concedes slowly and reluctantly.
  Urgency is applied to the seller, persona to the buyer, so the two manipulations sit on different sides of the negotiation and can be analyzed for interaction with language independently of each other.
- **Design:** a 2 (language) × 2 (urgency: none/strong) × 3 (persona: cooperative/neutral/headstrong) factorial = **12 conditions**, run on the same underlying scenarios, so every scenario is seen under all 12 conditions.
- **Scenario count and repetitions:** 12 scenarios sampled from CraigslistBargain (reduced from the original 20 to offset the added persona factor — see budget note below), each run under all 12 conditions, with each condition repeated **3 times** (temperature kept low for consistency, but 3 runs lets us distinguish a systematic effect from one-off randomness in a single conversation). This gives 12 × 12 × 3 = **432 negotiations**. At CraigslistBargain's average dialogue length (~9 turns), that's roughly 3,456 negotiation API calls, plus one FTA-coding call per full transcript (not per turn) for another ~432 calls — **around 3,888 total calls**, comfortably within Gemini's free tier (~1,500/day) across 2-3 days, matching the Aug 12–15 execution window already in the timeline.
- **Budget tradeoff, stated honestly:** going from 6 conditions (language × urgency only) to 12 conditions (adding persona) roughly doubles the design's complexity. To keep total API calls close to the original budget, scenario count was cut from 20 to 12 rather than cutting repetitions (3 repetitions were judged more important to keep, since detecting a *three-way interaction* — language × urgency × persona — needs some replication per cell to be trustworthy at all). This is a real statistical-power tradeoff worth naming directly in the paper: with only 12 scenarios × 3 repetitions per cell, this is an exploratory/pilot-scale factorial design, not one with the sample size to confidently detect a small three-way interaction — the honest framing is "we looked for evidence of this interaction," not "we definitively measured its size."
- **Turn limit / stopping rule:** cap each negotiation at a maximum of 10 turns; if no agreement is reached by then, log it as a failed/abandoned negotiation rather than letting the conversation run indefinitely.
- **Agent action structure:** each turn, the agent outputs both a natural-language utterance and an explicit structured action (offer / accept / reject / counter-offer, plus a price), mirroring the dataset's own `dialogue_acts` schema. This is what makes the setup a genuine agent rather than freeform dialogue role-play — every turn requires an explicit, parseable decision, not just a continuation of text. It also makes deal detection (has an agreement been reached?) a matter of parsing a structured field rather than guessing from free text.
- **Procedure (all via API calls, no training required):**
  1. Select 12 Craigslist scenarios (spread across the 6 item categories, since urgency and persona framings are both equally natural regardless of item type).
  2. Run each scenario as a full buyer-seller negotiation under each of the 12 (language × urgency × persona) conditions, keeping the scenario and target prices identical across conditions — only language, seller urgency, and buyer persona change.
  3. Two LLM instances (or API calls to the same model) play the buyer and seller roles, negotiating turn-by-turn (utterance + structured action each turn) until a deal is reached, one side walks away, or the 10-turn cap is hit.
  4. Log every turn: utterance, structured action, price offered (if any), turn number, and running state.
- **API / model choice:** run both negotiation agents on **Google Gemini API** (free tier, ~1,500 requests/day, no credit card, handles Korean well) as the primary model. For the generalizability check (rerunning core results on a second model, mentioned under Weaknesses), use **Qwen3** via **OpenRouter** — chosen over Llama because Qwen's training data has stronger representation of Asian languages, which matters for a Korean-language study specifically. OpenRouter's free tier caps at 50 requests/day, so plan on a small ($10) top-up (raises the cap to 1,000/day) once the Qwen replication run is scheduled, rather than trying to run the full replication on the free 50/day cap.
- **Translation and verification (lightweight, by design):** the actual translation volume is small and fixed — item categories/titles/descriptions come from only 6 categories, the urgency manipulation is 1 fixed sentence (strong only, per the 2-level design above), and the 3 persona instructions are also fixed, short text — so this doesn't need a full BenchMAX-style multi-round annotation process. Practical approach: machine-translate everything once, then do a single read-through pass over the full (small, fixed) set of urgency and persona phrasings and a spot-check of a handful of scenario descriptions per category, rather than verifying every single translated item individually. This is a deliberately lighter-weight version of the translate-then-verify pattern (see BenchMAX in References) — worth stating in the paper as a limitation (see below) rather than presenting it as equivalent-rigor verification.
- **Outcome analysis:** compare agreement rate, final price achieved (as a fraction of each side's target), and number of turns across the 12 conditions; specifically test (a) whether the *size* of the urgency effect (strong vs. none) differs by language, and (b) whether the *size* of the persona effect (headstrong vs. neutral, cooperative vs. neutral) differs by language — this second test is the language × persona interaction the added persona factor exists to check.
- **Cause analysis (not just outcome comparison) — explained simply:** A negotiation is full of moments where one side asks the other for something ("lower the price"), which can feel a little uncomfortable or pushy — linguists call this a **Face-Threatening Act (FTA)**: any utterance that puts some social pressure on the other person. The interesting part is that the *same* request can be phrased in very different ways, ranging from blunt to heavily cushioned. We'll code every turn in the negotiation into one of four categories, based on Brown & Levinson's classic politeness framework (1987). To keep this efficient, coding is done **once per full transcript, after the negotiation is complete** (the judge model sees the whole negotiation and labels every turn in a single call), not one API call per turn — this keeps the added coding cost small relative to the main negotiation runs, and means the judge is analyzing the finished interaction rather than influencing it as it happens. To avoid a model judging its own negotiation output, use a **different model as the judge** than whichever model(s) generated the negotiation (e.g., if Gemini negotiated, have Qwen do the FTA coding, or vice versa).

  | Category | What it means | Example (asking for $50 instead of the listed price) |
  |---|---|---|
  | **Bald on-record** | Straight up, no cushioning | *"Lower it to $50."* |
  | **Positive politeness** | Friendly framing, emphasizes closeness/goodwill | *"We can totally work something out — how about $50?"* |
  | **Negative politeness** | Cautious, deferential, apologetic | *"Sorry to ask, but would $50 be possible?"* |
  | **Off-record** | Indirect, just a hint, not a direct ask | *"Hmm, $50 would really help me out..."* |

  We'll have the judge tag every turn with one of these four labels (plus a human spot-check), for both the English and Korean transcripts, across all persona and urgency conditions. Then we compare: **does English lean more toward bald/direct requests, while Korean leans more toward negative-politeness/hedged requests (or vice versa)? And does this pattern hold the same way across all three personas, or does a "headstrong" buyer's directness look different in Korean than in English?** If we see a real difference in this distribution, and that difference lines up with the price/outcome differences we already found, that's a much more concrete "why" than just saying "Korean felt more polite."

- **One important caveat, worth knowing before we build on this:** Brown & Levinson's theory was developed mostly from Western language data, and other researchers (e.g., work on Chinese "face") have argued it doesn't fully capture how face and politeness work in East Asian languages — Korean included. A concrete example of the concern: B&L's framework assumes indirectness = more polite, but in some East Asian contexts, *very* indirect requests toward someone of higher status can actually be read as distant or evasive rather than polite, which complicates a simple "more indirect = more polite" reading. Because of this, we'll also bring in **Ting-Toomey's Face-Negotiation Theory (1985)**, which was built specifically for comparing face/politeness *across* cultures rather than assuming one culture's pattern is universal. Practically, this just means: when we write up the Korean-vs-English results, we frame it as "here's what we found, interpreted through both frameworks," rather than assuming B&L's Western-derived categories are a neutral, universal yardstick.

## Strengths (from an acceptance standpoint)

- **Clear, novel independent variable, now crossed with a well-studied one:** language-as-variable is a genuinely underexplored angle (see Related Work check below); crossing it with persona (a well-studied variable in this literature) lets the study ask a sharper question — not "does persona matter" (already shown) but "does persona matter *the same way* across languages" — which is a more precise, harder-to-dismiss contribution than either factor alone.
- **Genuine agent, not just role-play:** the structured action output (offer/accept/reject/counter-offer + price, alongside the utterance) means the model makes an explicit, parseable decision every turn — this heads off a reasonable reviewer objection that the setup is closer to dialogue role-play than to an agent making decisions.
- **The urgency dimension adds a mechanism, not just an outcome:** rather than just "results differ by language," the study can speak to *how* — whether a real-world-relevant social signal (a counterpart in a difficult situation) is processed differently depending on the language of interaction. This is closer to the mechanism-oriented framing that similar workshops' example papers (theory of mind, moral dilemmas — see Related Work) tend to favor over pure outcome comparisons.
- **Turnkey dataset:** CraigslistBargain is a well-established, widely-cited benchmark, so results are easy to contextualize against prior negotiation work.
- **Fully API-based:** no model training required, which fits a tight workshop deadline.
- **Direct alignment with the CFP:** matches "Agent-Based Social Simulation... coordination, conflict, cooperation" and "Persona Fidelity and Population Heterogeneity... demographic variation" from the SocialAgent call for papers — the persona addition in particular now speaks directly to the "Population Heterogeneity" language in the CFP.
- **Practical relevance:** speaks directly to real deployment questions for multilingual customer-facing or negotiation agents, and to a genuinely underexplored fairness question: does the *same* stated hardship, or the *same* configured persona, get expressed differently by an AI negotiator depending on the language it operates in?

## Weaknesses / Limitations (being honest, from an acceptance standpoint)

- **Confound risk between "language" and "translation/data availability":** the model may simply have seen less negotiation-style training data in non-English languages, which could produce outcome differences that look like a "language effect" but are really a "training data volume effect." This is hard to fully rule out with only two languages and should be stated explicitly as a limitation rather than glossed over.
- **The urgency and persona conditions are both researcher-constructed, not drawn from data:** since CraigslistBargain has neither field, both are experimental manipulations added on top of real scenarios. The method section must state this plainly rather than let it read as if either came from the dataset.
- **The design is now underpowered for its own most interesting question:** the whole point of adding persona was to test a three-way interaction (language × urgency × persona), but with only 12 scenarios × 3 repetitions per cell across 12 conditions, this is genuinely a small pilot sample for detecting a three-way interaction reliably. This should be stated as an honest limitation — the paper can report what it finds and flag it as suggestive/exploratory, but shouldn't overclaim statistical confidence in a three-way interaction effect at this sample size.
- **Dropping the "weak" urgency level to 2 levels total (none/strong) trades away some resolution:** the original 3-level urgency design could distinguish gradual from step-function effects; the 2-level version can only say "urgency changed things" not "the size of urgency mattered." This was a deliberate tradeoff to make room for the persona factor within the same API budget — worth being upfront about in the paper rather than presenting the 2-level design as the original plan.
- **Judge-model style scoring needs its own validation:** if the same judge model is used to score directness/politeness across languages, its own possible cross-lingual bias could distort the style analysis. This isn't a hypothetical concern — Chung & Freienthal (2026, see References) directly show LLM judges are unstable specifically on pragmatic/discourse-level judgments (as opposed to surface metrics) across morphologically rich languages, which is exactly the kind of judgment this study's FTA coding requires. A small manual spot-check of judge scores by a fluent speaker of each language is planned to address this, but should be reported honestly as a partial mitigation, not a full solution.
- **Translation verification is lightweight, not exhaustive:** since the goal is to keep translation effort low, only a spot-check (not full item-by-item verification) is performed on the Korean text (scenarios, urgency phrasing, and now persona instructions). This is a reasonable tradeoff given the small, fixed translation surface, but it should be stated explicitly as a limitation — a reviewer could reasonably ask whether every piece of Korean text reads naturally, and the honest answer is "spot-checked, not fully verified."
- **Single-annotator verification:** with only myself doing the Korean spot-check, there's no independent second-rater check on translation quality (unlike the multi-annotator designs used in BenchMAX or similar). Worth naming as a limitation rather than presenting the verification as more rigorous than it is.

- **He, H., Chen, D., Balakrishnan, A., & Liang, P. (2018). Decoupling Strategy and Generation in Negotiation Dialogues.** arXiv:1808.09637. *The original paper introducing the CraigslistBargain dataset this study uses — read this to understand how the scenarios and target prices are structured.*

End-to-End Learning of Negotiation Dialogues.** arXiv:1706.05125. *An alternative, simpler negotiation dataset/framework — useful background even though this study uses CraigslistBargain, since it's the other major reference point in LLM negotiation research.*
- **"AgreeMate: Teaching LLMs to Haggle."** arXiv:2412.18690. *A recent paper using LLMs directly on CraigslistBargain — useful for seeing current baselines and typical evaluation metrics (agreement rate, price achieved) on this exact dataset.*
- **"Better To Ask in English? Evaluating Factual Accuracy of Multilingual LLMs in English and Low-Resource Languages."** arXiv:2504.20022. *Not about negotiation, but directly relevant background: shows that LLM behavior and quality can differ substantially by language even holding task and model constant — the same kind of language-as-variable logic this study applies to negotiation instead of factual QA.*
- **Moradshahi, M., Shen, T., Bali, K., Choudhury, M., et al. (2023). X-RiSAWOZ: High-Quality End-to-End Multilingual Dialogue Datasets and Few-shot Agents.** arXiv:2306.17674. *The closest existing multilingual (including Korean and Hindi) dialogue dataset, though task-oriented rather than negotiation-based. Cited here for its translation-plus-human-verification methodology, which this study's Korean (and third-language) translation of CraigslistBargain should follow rather than relying on raw machine translation alone.*
- **"BenchMAX: A Comprehensive Multilingual Evaluation Suite for Large Language Models."** arXiv:2502.07346. *Directly supports this study's translate-then-verify methodology: describes a multi-round process where native-speaking annotators post-edit machine-translated samples, cross-checked with automatic verifiers. Cite this in the Method section as the methodological basis for the researcher-led (rather than crowdsourced) translation verification approach used here.*
- **He, H. (2018) et al., in dialogue with Chen, D. (2022), "Quality at a Glance: An Audit of Web-Crawled Multilingual Datasets."** arXiv:2103.12028. *Relevant background on translation/annotation quality auditing methodology — useful for framing why manual verification of translated urgency phrasings matters, not just for full-dialogue translation.*
- **He He (2024) et al., "AgreeMate"-adjacent: Hua, Y., Qu, L., & Haffari, G. (2024). Assistive Large Language Model Agents for Socially-Aware Negotiation Dialogues.** arXiv:2402.01737. *An example of LLM-vs-LLM negotiation role-play used as a research methodology (with a third "remediator" agent) — useful for seeing how this workshop community frames multi-agent negotiation studies methodologically, distinct from this study's language-as-variable angle.*
- **"LLMs with Personalities in Multi-issue Negotiation Games."** (Accepted, SocialLLM @ ICWSM 2026 / Social Agents in the Wild @ ACL 2026.) *The closest related paper found in this workshop's sister editions — studies persona (not language) as the variable affecting negotiation outcomes. Worth reading to see how a closely related paper in the same venue lineage frames its contribution and to make sure this study's framing is clearly differentiated (language rather than persona as the manipulated variable).*
- **Brown, P., & Levinson, S. C. (1987). Politeness: Some Universals in Language Usage.** Cambridge University Press. *The core theoretical framework for the cause-analysis coding scheme (FTA identification and mitigation-strategy classification). Essential reading before designing the coding scheme.*
- **Ting-Toomey, S. (1985). Toward a Theory of Conflict and Culture.** *Developed specifically for cross-cultural comparison, unlike Brown & Levinson — cite this alongside B&L to address the Western-centrism critique when interpreting English-Korean differences.*
- **"Keeping Up Appearances: Computational Modeling of Face Acts in Persuasion Oriented Discussions."** arXiv:2009.10815. *A direct precedent for computationally coding FTAs and face-acts in dialogue — read this to see how the coding scheme was operationalized in an NLP pipeline, as a template for this study's judge-model-based coding approach.*
- **Yin, Z., Wang, H., Horio, K., Kawahara, D., & Sekine, S. (2024). Should We Respect LLMs? A Cross-Lingual Study on the Influence of Prompt Politeness on LLM Performance.** SICon 2024 (ACL Anthology). *Finds that the effect of prompt politeness on LLM task performance is language-dependent (English/Chinese/Japanese) — closely related to this study's premise, but studies politeness as an input affecting performance rather than as an outcome shaped by language; worth citing to clearly distinguish this study's contribution.*
- **"No Universal Courtesy: A Cross-Linguistic, Multi-Model Study of Politeness Effects on LLMs Using the PLUM Corpus."** arXiv:2604.16275 (2026). *Extends the Yin et al. finding across English/Hindi/Spanish and multiple model families, releasing the PLUM corpus; strong recent evidence that politeness effects on LLMs are neither universal nor model-independent — good supporting citation for this study's core premise that language shapes LLM social behavior.*
- **Chung, I., & Freienthal, L. (2026). Cross-Lingual Stability of LLM Judges Under Controlled Generation: Evidence from Finno-Ugric Languages.** arXiv:2602.02287. *Finds LLM-judge reliability specifically degrades on pragmatic/discourse-level judgments (not surface metrics) across morphologically rich languages — direct evidence supporting this study's planned manual spot-check of judge-coded FTA labels; cite in the Weaknesses section as concrete grounding, not just a generic caution.*
